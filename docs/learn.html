Learn the VRP: models, variants, heuristics and local-search operators

A compact, referenced guide to the capacitated vehicle routing problem (CVRP) and its multi-depot variant (MDVRP): the mathematical model, the variant landscape, the heuristics used to solve them, every local-search operator implemented in this repository (with animated before/after diagrams) and the benchmark datasets. This page contains no experimental results: run your own, with several seeds, in the Solver tab. Bracketed numbers [n] point to the reference list; every DOI there was checked against Crossref.

1. The problem and its mathematical model

Given a depot, a set N = {1,…,n} of customers with demands qi, a homogeneous fleet of vehicles of capacity Q, and a travel cost cij for every pair of locations, the CVRP asks for a set of routes that each start and end at the depot, serve every customer exactly once, respect the capacity, and minimise the total travel cost. The problem was introduced as the "truck dispatching problem" [8] and generalises the travelling salesman problem, hence it is NP-hard [13]. Overviews: [11, 26].

1.1 Two-index vehicle-flow formulation (the reference model)

Let xij = 1 if a vehicle drives directly from i to j, and let ui be the cumulative load after serving i (Miller–Tucker–Zemlin-type constraints [14] remove sub-tours and enforce capacity at once). Node 0 is the depot and m the fleet size.

min   Σ_{i,j ∈ {0}∪N, i≠j}  c_ij · x_ij
s.t.  Σ_j x_ij = 1                          ∀ i ∈ N        (one successor)
      Σ_i x_ij = 1                          ∀ j ∈ N        (one predecessor)
      Σ_j x_0j ≤ m                                          (fleet size)
      u_j ≥ u_i + q_j − Q (1 − x_ij)        ∀ i,j ∈ N, i≠j (load propagation, no sub-tours)
      q_i ≤ u_i ≤ Q                         ∀ i ∈ N        (capacity)
      x_ij ∈ {0,1}

Interactive: pick a view to see what one family of constraints means on a toy instance (8 customers, Q = 22, m = 3). Hand-made illustration, not a result.

This model is compact but its linear relaxation is weak. It is stated here as the reference definition; this project does not hand it to a MILP solver. The heuristics were instead checked on tiny instances (n ≤ 10) against a subset dynamic programme that returns the same optimum (see the exact methods in section 4). A route-duration limit D adds cumulative-time variables of the same kind.

1.2 Set-partitioning formulation (basis of the best exact methods)

With Ω the set of all feasible routes, cr the cost of route r and air = 1 if r visits i [3]:

min  Σ_{r∈Ω} c_r λ_r     s.t.  Σ_{r∈Ω} a_ir λ_r = 1  ∀ i ∈ N,    Σ_{r∈Ω} λ_r ≤ m,    λ_r ∈ {0,1}

Ω is exponentially large, so branch-cut-and-price algorithms generate routes by column generation and strengthen the bound with cuts [16]; the generic solver of [17] applies the same machinery to many variants. These methods are state of the art for exact solution but are still limited to a few hundred customers, which is why heuristics (section 4) are used in practice.

Real problems add attributes to the CVRP. Surveys that organise them are [4, 29, 26]; [30] shows how one metaheuristic framework can address many attribute combinations.

3. Single depot vs multi-depot

The CVRP is the special case t = 1 of the MDVRP with t depots, and the Solver is written for the general case: nothing in the data model, the local search or the exact model is special-cased for a single depot. The multi-depot structure nevertheless changes what a "move" may do, which is the point of this section.

3.1 Multi-depot model

Besides the customer-to-customer arcs xij, use ydj (a route leaves depot d for customer j), zid (a route returns from i to d) and an assignment variable aid = 1 if customer i is served from depot d. This is the model whose optimum the verification programme of section 4.D reproduces on tiny instances (depot-dependent capacity Qd and fleet md included; the verifier is a set-partitioning dynamic programme, not a MILP solver):

min   Σ c_ij x_ij + Σ_{d,j} c_dj y_dj + Σ_{i,d} c_id z_id
s.t.  Σ_i x_ij + Σ_d y_dj = 1      ∀ j ∈ N          (one predecessor)
      Σ_j x_ij + Σ_d z_id = 1      ∀ i ∈ N          (one successor)
      Σ_d a_id = 1                 ∀ i ∈ N          (every customer has one depot)
      y_dj ≤ a_jd ,  z_jd ≤ a_jd                    (a route is attached to its depot at both ends)
      a_jd ≥ a_id + x_ij − 1       ∀ i≠j, d         (consecutive customers share the depot)
      Σ_j y_dj ≤ m_d               ∀ d              (fleet per depot)
      u_j ≥ u_i + q_j − Q_max (1 − x_ij) ,  q_i ≤ u_i ≤ Σ_d Q_d a_id

Interactive: toy instance with two depots and 11 customers. Change the fleet and capacity of each depot, or look at a route that ends at the wrong depot.

The coupling constraints (ajd ≥ …) are exactly what a single-depot model does not need: they force the first and the last depot of a route to coincide.

3.3 Assign first, or search jointly?

Fixing the depot of every customer to its nearest depot and then solving one CVRP per depot is the natural "cluster first, route second" approach; it is a simplified version of the assignment step of constructions such as the multi-terminal sweep of Gillett and Johnson [9]. Its weakness is structural: the assignment ignores the per-depot fleet and capacity limits, so it can overload a depot (even making the instance infeasible for that assignment), and customers near the border between two depots are often served more cheaply from the other one. Letting the search move customers, routes and whole depot assignments (the moves of section 5.3) removes that restriction at the price of a larger neighbourhood and a more involved feasibility bookkeeping. The Solver tab lets you try the construction heuristics and operator sets (with and without the depot moves) on any of the instances.

Scope of the Solver. Its engine is a teaching-grade JavaScript implementation that runs in your browser. It is not tuned to reproduce published results and is much less engineered than the solvers above. Its value is transparency: every solution it reports is re-checked for feasibility, and its methods are tested for feasibility, determinism and for never going below proven optima or published best-known values.

D. Exact methods (for reference)

Set partitioning [3] with branch-cut-and-price [16]; generic exact solver [17]. A subset dynamic programme (Held–Karp per route, combined over customer subsets and depots; n ≤ 14) was used in this project to verify the heuristics on tiny instances; it is not competitive and is not part of the Solver.

E. The 20 search methods of the Solver

The Solver tab offers the twenty methods below on every instance, and its compare several methods option runs a chosen set of them on the same instance with the same seeds. All of them share the instance model, the feasibility rules and, where they use local search, the operators of section 5, so differences between methods come from the search strategy and not from the plumbing. Each method is written from the cited literature in a compact, route-level form. They are teaching-grade re-implementations, not reproductions of the published codes, and their parameters are not tuned to match published results.

"One step" is the unit counted as one iteration in the convergence plot. Because a step of a population method (one child) and a step of a trajectory method (one random move) cost very different amounts of time, iteration counts are comparable within a method but not across methods. The compare option therefore gives every method the same compute time: a short timing probe converts the time budget into an iteration budget for each method, which also fixes the cooling or adaptation schedules of the methods that depend on the number of iterations (simulated annealing, ALNS, SISR-style search). The calibration is approximate, particularly when several browser workers share a few CPU cores.

Reading the table. All twenty methods run in the browser Solver. The random-key family (BRKGA, PSO, DE) searches the unit hypercube and decodes keys into giant tours with the optimal split of Prins [Prins 2004]. Random-key random search is not a published method: it is the control baseline of that family, and any random-key method that does not beat it is not using its population. GRASP builds its starts with a randomised nearest-neighbour construction (section 4.A, restricted candidate list); multi-start local search decodes random priority keys with the optimal split.

5. Local-search operators

All operators are applied inside one descent loop: take a customer u from a queue of "dirty" customers; for each of its k nearest neighbours v (granular neighbourhood [25]) evaluate every move variant that involves u and v; apply the best improving one; put the customers of the touched routes back in the queue; stop when the queue is empty. Fleet limits are penalised, not forbidden.

O(1) evaluation. Efficient implementations keep, for every route, prefix sums of demand, service time and edge cost. A candidate route is then a concatenation of at most a few pieces "route r, positions i..j, optionally reversed", whose cost, load and duration are obtained from the prefix sums without touching the nodes (the idea used by [22] for edge exchanges). The Solver rebuilds the two affected routes instead (O(route length)), which is slower but simpler to verify.

Diagrams: blue = route 1, orange = route 2, gold dots = customers, black squares = depots, ringed customers are the ones whose edges change. Press ▶ Play (or drag the slider): the edges the move removes turn dashed red and fade out, then the edges it adds are drawn in green and take the colour of their route. The "after" routes are written out from each operator's definition; the operators themselves are verified in the test suite.

5.1 Customer moves

Relocate SD + MD

Remove a customer (or a segment) and insert it before/after a neighbour v, in the same or in another route. Fast solvers also move short segments of 2–3 customers, optionally reversed; the Solver moves single customers (segments are handled by Or-opt). Also opens a new route when that is cheaper and the fleet allows.

Source family: [22].

Or-opt SD + MD

Move a segment of 2–3 consecutive customers, reversed or not, to another position of the same or another route. Here the segment (a2,a3) is inserted reversed.

In the Solver, Or-opt moves segments within a route and between routes.

Swap SD + MD

Exchange two customers u, v of different routes: each takes the other's position. Capacity and duration of both routes are re-checked.

CROSS-exchange SD + MD

Exchange two segments of consecutive customers between two routes (here two customers each). The Solver swaps single customers only.

[24]

2-opt (intra-route) SD + MD

Reverse a sub-sequence of one route, replacing two edges by two others. Capacity is unaffected; with a duration limit it is re-checked.

5.2 Route-pair moves

2-opt* (tail exchange) SD + MD

Cut route A after u and route B after v and exchange the tails: A = head(A) + tail(B), B = head(B) + tail(A). Valid for any pair of routes, also of different depots (each route keeps its own depot).

[19]

2-opt* with reversal same depot only

A = head(A) + reversed head(B), B = reversed tail(A) + tail(B). Each new route would run from the depot to a customer and back along reversed halves; with two different depots the routes would start at one depot and end at the other, so the operator is only generated when A and B share their depot.

SWAP* SD + MD

Swap u ∈ A and v ∈ B, but insert each at its cheapest position in the other route (found from the three best insertion positions kept per customer and route), not necessarily where the other customer was. Evaluated for pairs of routes whose customers are close to each other.

[31] · described from the literature; not in the Solver.

5.3 Depot moves (multi-depot only)

Relocate across depots MD

The ordinary relocate becomes an assignment change when the target route belongs to another depot: the customer leaves depot D1's route and joins one of D2. No special code is required because every delta uses the route's own depot nodes.

Route re-homing MD

Keep the customer sequence of a route but attach it to another depot (both depot edges change). Accepted if capacity, duration and fleet of the new depot allow it and the cost decreases.

Teaching operator of this project; not claimed to originate from the literature.

Depot swap MD

Exchange the depots of two routes that belong to different depots, keeping each sequence. Useful when two routes are "crossed" with respect to their depots and neither single re-homing is feasible because of the fleet limits.

Teaching operator (depot_swap).

New route at the nearest depot MD

Move a customer into a fresh route started at one of its two nearest depots that still has an unused vehicle. This lets the search use idle fleet capacity in a depot that currently has few routes.

Part of relocate.

5.4 Operator sets (presets)

The local search can be run with any subset of the operators. The Solver (Expert mode) offers basic (relocate, swap, 2-opt, 2-opt*), standard (adds Or-opt) and full (adds the depot moves), plus a custom selection.

7. Open code, open data and further research

Open source solvers worth reading

  • HGS-CVRP (MIT): C++ hybrid genetic search with SWAP*, canonical CVRP, also handles asymmetric distances and duration limits, designed for up to about 1,000 customers [31].
  • PyVRP (MIT): Python/C++ solver, HGS-based, supports time windows, heterogeneous fleets and multiple depots among others [32].
  • FILO (AGPL-3.0; a more scalable FILO2 is linked from its README): fast ILS for large CVRPs [1].
  • ALNS (Python): a documented ALNS framework with several acceptance criteria and operator-selection schemes, with VRP and TSP examples [Wouda & Lan 2023].
  • BRKGA-MP-IPR (C++, header-only; Python and Julia versions exist): the multi-parent biased random-key GA with implicit path relinking; you supply only a decoder [Andrade et al. 2021].

Open data

CVRPLib (X, XML, XL and classical sets with BKS), NEO and STOM-Group for the MDVRP, SINTEF TOP for time-window benchmarks (links in section 6).

Further research directions

These are our synthesis of gaps visible in this project and the material above. We have not systematically reviewed the "future work" sections of the cited papers, so none of the points is attributed to them.
  • Equal-time comparison of the 20 methods. Compare the methods of section 4.E at matched compute time on the X and Cordeau sets with at least 10 seeds, then tune each with an automatic configurator on a training subset and re-test on held-out instances: how much of the ranking survives tuning is the open question. The random-key control (RKRS) is the yardstick for that family.
  • Budget-dependent schedules. Methods whose cooling or adaptation schedule is tied to the iteration budget (simulated annealing, ALNS, SISR-style) behave differently under a time limit than under an iteration limit; a systematic study of time-based schedules against calibrated iteration budgets is missing here.
  • MDVRP benchmarking with verified references. Collect primary-source best-known values for p01–p23 and pr01–pr10, then report gaps with at least 10 seeds and matched run times against HGS-style or PyVRP baselines.
  • Depot-aware SWAP*. SWAP* can be applied between routes of any depots; whether its best-insertion shortcuts (top-three positions) stay exact when capacities and depot edges differ per depot deserves a dedicated test and measurement.
  • Fleet-tight multi-depot instances. Nearest-depot assignment can violate the per-depot fleet limits when fleets are tight (section 3.3); a study of assignment strategies under tight fleets (bin-packing-aware assignment) is open.
  • Depot-level operators. Route re-homing and depot swap only act on routes whose depot is not the best one for them, and relocation across depots already re-assigns single customers (section 5.3), so it is an open question when they matter in practice. Instances with depot-dependent capacities or very uneven fleets, and perturbation-based searches, are the places to check whether they ever matter.
  • Extending the model. Time windows (MDVRPTW), periodic visits (MDPVRP) and heterogeneous fleets require resource-extension style feasibility checks.
